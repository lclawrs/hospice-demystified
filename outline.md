## Narrative Arc

> *"Hospice is not giving up. It's giving more — more comfort, more dignity, more time for what matters. Your referral is what makes that possible."*

The talk moves through a classic problem → solution arc:
1. Frame the problem (underuse + late referral)
2. Teach identification (who, when, how)
3. Teach communication (how to have the conversation)
4. Teach the continuum (palliative care ≠ hospice)
5. Teach the end-of-life moment (clinical + human)
6. Demystify the system (what hospice actually IS)
7. Empower action (you can do this in your practice)

## SECTION 1 — The Problem: Hospice is Underused (Slides 1–5)

### Slide 1 — Title
- **"Hospice Demystified"**
- Dr. Michael Haas, MD | LAFP 79th Annual Assembly | July 2026
- Optional: powerful, quiet image — a hand hold, a sunset, something human

### Slide 2 — Opening Hook
- A brief clinical vignette: patient dies in ICU on a vent, family in shock, no one had the conversation
- OR: stat-led hook — "Last year, more than half of Medicare beneficiaries who died enrolled in hospice — but many for just 3 to 7 days."
- Question to the room: *"How many of you have had a patient you wish you'd referred sooner?"*

### Slide 3 — The Utilization Problem
- ~52% of Medicare decedents used hospice in 2023 (MedPAC 2025)
- Median length of stay: 18 days — but the distribution is heavily skewed toward the very end of life
- Approximately 15% of patients die within 7 days of enrollment
- **The gap:** Patients who could benefit for months are only getting days
- Source: MedPAC March 2025 Report to Congress, Ch. 9; NHPCO Facts & Figures 2024

### Slide 4 — Why Does This Happen?
- Physician barriers: prognosis uncertainty, fear of "taking away hope," discomfort with the conversation
- System barriers: late referral culture, confusing eligibility, fragmented care
- Patient/family barriers: misconceptions ("hospice = giving up," fear of morphine, cultural)
- **Key insight:** The #1 reason for late referral is physician hesitation — which means *we* are the fix

### Slide 5 — What's at Stake
- Early hospice enrollment → better pain control, better family satisfaction, fewer hospitalizations
- **Temel et al., NEJM 2010**: Early palliative care in metastatic NSCLC → better quality of life, less aggressive EOL care, AND longer survival (11.6 vs. 8.9 months)
- The counterintuitive truth: patients who receive palliative care earlier sometimes live longer
- Source: Temel JS et al. NEJM. 2010;363(8):733–742.

## SECTION 2 — Objective 1: Identifying the Right Patients (Slides 6–10)

### Slide 6 — Medicare Hospice Eligibility: The Basics
- Physician certifies: prognosis ≤ 6 months if illness runs its normal course
- Patient elects comfort-focused care (waives curative treatment for the terminal illness)
- Two 90-day benefit periods, then unlimited 60-day periods — recertification required
- **Common myth to bust:** "Once you're on hospice you can't get off." (False — patients can and do revoke at any time per 42 CFR 418.28)

### Slide 7 — The Surprise Question
- *"Would you be surprised if this patient died within the next 12 months?"*
- If the answer is **No** → this patient deserves a palliative care conversation NOW
- Simple, validated, evidence-supported screening tool
- Source: PCNOW Fast Fact #360; BMJ Supportive & Palliative Care. 2022;12(2):211–217

### Slide 8 — Disease-Specific Triggers
A quick-reference matrix:
| Condition | Referral Triggers |
|---|---|
| Cancer | Stage IV + declining performance status |
| CHF | NYHA Class III/IV despite optimal therapy; repeated hospitalizations |
| COPD | FEV1 <25%, requiring home O2, declining functional status |
| Dementia | FAST Stage 7: unable to walk, dress, or bathe; recurrent infections |
| Renal Failure | Creatinine clearance <15 mL/min, declining, refusing dialysis |
| Liver Disease | Child-Pugh Class C; refractory ascites; hepatic encephalopathy |

### Slide 9 — Prognostic Tools
- **PPS (Palliative Performance Scale):** validated functional status tool with established mortality correlation
- **ECOG Performance Status:** widely familiar, maps to prognosis and functional decline
- **Surprise Question + PCST (Palliative Care Screening Tool):** combined use outperforms either alone for predicting 12-month mortality (c-statistic 0.739; p<0.001)
- Source: BMJ Supp Pall Care. 2022;12(2):211–217
- Practical message: *Use the tools, trust your gut, AND ask the surprise question — together they're stronger than any one alone*

### Slide 10 — Common Non-Cancer Diagnoses (Often Missed)
- Heart failure, COPD, dementia, renal failure, liver cirrhosis — all hospice-eligible
- Family physicians own these patients — they are often the ONLY one who can initiate this
- "You are the gatekeeper. No one else in their care team has the relationship you have."

## SECTION 3 — Objective 2: The Conversation (Slides 11–15)

### Slide 11 — Why We Avoid the Conversation
- "I don't want to take away hope"
- "The family isn't ready"
- "I don't have time"
- "They'll think I'm giving up on them"
- Reality check: *Patients with serious illness want to know. Studies consistently show patients desire more prognostic information the sicker they become.*

### Slide 12 — Palliative Care ≠ Giving Up
- Reframe: it's adding a layer of support, not removing treatment
- Can be concurrent with disease-directed therapy (early palliative care model)
- Language that works: "We're going to focus on making sure you feel as good as possible, for as long as possible."

### Slide 13 — REMAP Framework (Goals of Care)
**R** — Reframe (set up the conversation: "I want to make sure your care matches your values and priorities")  
**E** — Expect emotion (make space; don't rush past the feeling)  
**M** — Map out patient values and goals ("What's most important to you?")  
**A** — Align with those values ("Based on what you've told me…")  
**P** — Propose a plan (connect their values to a concrete next step)  
Source: Childers JW et al. J Oncol Pract. 2017;13(10):e844–e850.

### Slide 14 — SPIKES for Delivering Bad News
**S** — Setting (private, seated, no interruptions)  
**P** — Perception (what does the patient already know/believe?)  
**I** — Invitation (does the patient want information? how much?)  
**K** — Knowledge (share news clearly, without jargon, in chunks)  
**E** — Emotions (respond to emotions first, before more information)  
**S** — Strategy/Summary (next steps, plan, follow-up)  
Source: Baile WF et al. Oncologist. 2000;5(4):302–311.

### Slide 15 — Practical Tips for the Office
- Schedule a dedicated "goals of care" visit — don't tack it onto a 15-min med check
- Invite family to be present
- Use teach-back: "Can you tell me in your own words what we talked about today?"
- Document in the chart — shared decision-making conversation, patient preferences, surrogate designation

## SECTION 4 — Objective 3: Palliative Care Across the Continuum (Slides 16–19)

### Slide 16 — The Critical Distinction
[DIAGRAM SLIDE]
- Timeline from diagnosis → death
- **Palliative care** starts at diagnosis, runs the full course
- **Hospice** is a specific program that begins when curative intent ends and prognosis ≤ 6 months
- "Hospice is one type of palliative care. Palliative care is not just hospice."

### Slide 17 — Symptoms Across the Continuum
- Pain, fatigue, dyspnea, nausea, anxiety, depression — present throughout, not just at end
- Caregiver burden is often the most underaddressed issue
- Social determinants: finances, housing instability, isolation — all affect outcomes
- Spiritual distress — often unspoken; often the most important thing to the patient

### Slide 18 — The Interdisciplinary Approach
- Medicine alone is not enough
- The hospice/palliative team: physician, NP, nurse, social worker, chaplain, home health aide, volunteer, bereavement counselor
- Family physician's role: quarterback, not solo player
- "You don't have to do all of this alone — that's what the team is for"

### Slide 19 — Advance Care Planning
- POLST/MOLST forms — actionable medical orders
- Advance directives — legal documents
- Surrogate designation — who speaks if the patient can't?
- **Pearl:** The time to have this conversation is *before* the crisis, not during it
- Family physicians are uniquely positioned to do this over time, across visits

## SECTION 5 — Objective 4: Imminent Death — What to Expect (Slides 20–23)

### Slide 20 — Recognizing the Final Days
Clinical signs of imminent death (days to hours):
- Decreased or absent oral intake
- Mottled, cool, or cyanotic extremities
- Cheyne-Stokes breathing; pooled secretions ("death rattle")
- Decreased urine output, dark urine
- Decreased consciousness; eyes partially open
- "This is a normal dying process. This is not suffering."

### Slide 21 — Comfort Medications at End of Life
Anticipatory prescribing — the standard comfort kit:
- **Morphine or oxycodone** — pain and dyspnea (SL or SQ when oral route is lost)
- **Lorazepam or midazolam** — anxiety, terminal agitation
- **Haloperidol** — delirium, agitation
- **Glycopyrrolate or hyoscine (scopolamine)** — secretion management ("death rattle")
- Source: NICE Clinical Guideline — Care of Dying Adults in the Last Days of Life. 2015. (NCBI Bookshelf: NBK356005)
- **Key message:** These medications treat suffering. When dosed appropriately for symptom management, they do not hasten death. (Principle of double effect — well-established in medical ethics and law.)

### Slide 22 — What to Tell the Family
- Normalize the process: describe what they'll see and what it means
- "The body's hunger and thirst naturally diminish. Forcing fluids at this stage can actually increase discomfort."
- Hearing is believed to be the last sense to go — encourage family to keep talking, to say what needs to be said
- It's okay to say goodbye. It's okay to give permission to let go.
- After death: call hospice first — not 911

### Slide 23 — Bereavement Care
- Medicare requires hospice to provide bereavement services for a minimum of 13 months following the patient's death (42 CFR 418.88)
- Complicated grief is real — family physicians should watch for it at follow-up visits
- "Your job doesn't end when the patient dies. The family is still your patient."

## SECTION 6 — Objective 5: How Hospice Works (Slides 24–28)

### Slide 24 — The Four Levels of Medicare Hospice Care
1. **Routine Home Care** — standard level; IDT visits, medications, and equipment delivered to the home
2. **Continuous Home Care** — crisis management; continuous nursing care at home during a brief period of acute crisis (minimum 8 hours/day, primarily nursing)
3. **General Inpatient Care (GIP)** — for acute symptoms that cannot be managed at home; short-term, inpatient facility setting
4. **Respite Care** — up to 5 consecutive days inpatient to provide the caregiver a rest
Source: CMS Medicare Benefit Policy Manual, Chapter 9; 42 CFR 418.108

### Slide 25 — What's Covered Under the Medicare Hospice Benefit
- Nursing visits (RN + LPN)
- Physician services (hospice medical director)
- Social work, chaplaincy, volunteer services
- Home health aide (personal care)
- Medications related to the terminal diagnosis — 5% coinsurance, capped at $5 per prescription during home care; $0 during inpatient care
- Durable medical equipment: hospital bed, wheelchair, commode, O2, etc.
- Bereavement counseling (13 months)
- **What's NOT covered:** treatments aimed at curing the terminal illness

### Slide 26 — How to Refer
Step-by-step for the family physician:
1. Identify the patient (Surprise Question + eligibility criteria)
2. Have the conversation (REMAP)
3. Contact your local hospice agency — they conduct the intake assessment
4. Sign the certification of terminal illness (you do not need to become the hospice physician)
5. Stay involved — you remain the attending physician if you choose
- Tip: build a relationship with one or two local hospice agencies; know who to call before you need to

### Slide 27 — Hospice and Your Practice
- Myth: "Hospice takes the patient away from me."
- Reality: You can remain the attending physician throughout. Hospice supports your patient and your practice.
- Benefit: reduces urgent after-hours calls, crisis calls, and unnecessary ER visits
- Better symptom management = better patient and family satisfaction
- **The moral case:** Patients deserve to die with dignity, at home, with the people they love, without unnecessary suffering. Your referral is what makes that possible.

### Slide 28 — Call to Action / Key Takeaways
One takeaway per objective:
1. **Ask the Surprise Question** at every serious illness visit
2. **Use REMAP** — you have the framework; now practice it
3. **Palliative care starts at diagnosis** — don't wait for hospice eligibility
4. **Know the signs** — prepare families in advance; comfort kits prevent crisis calls
5. **Hospice is a team** — refer early, stay involved, let the team support your patient
